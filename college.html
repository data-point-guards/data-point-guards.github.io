The Pipeline

Does March Madness stardom survive the jump?

“How does NCAA tournament success translate into a WNBA career?”

College success makes a player visible, but it does not answer what happens after draft night. Research on WNBA selection finds that scoring and Final Four experience can improve draft position, while draft position itself explains only a small share of later professional performance (Harris and Berri 2015). The qualities that attract attention in March are therefore not identical to the qualities that sustain a professional career.

From promise to opportunity

The transition is also shaped by access. First-round selections are less likely to leave the league or take a career break, which may reflect both ability and the longer opportunity teams give to players in whom they have invested (Jepsen 2023). Injury can narrow that opportunity further: one matched study found substantially shorter WNBA careers among players who entered the league with a prior ACL reconstruction (Tramer et al. 2021).

These findings change how we read the college-to-pro pipeline. A short professional career does not automatically prove that a college player was overrated, just as a long career does not result from college fame alone. Draft position, roster scarcity, health, team fit, and repeated chances all sit between college performance and the professional record.

How this connects to our data

The next page follows the first twenty selections from the 2020 WNBA Draft into the 2025 season. That comparison does not turn team wins into an individual grade. The draft is a forecast; a career is the much longer test.
