The Record

Who gets to be called great?

“How does the dataset’s focus on rankings, awards, and team statistics influence the way we define ‘greatness’ in women’s basketball?”

A career award total looks objective because it is a count. Yet the count begins only after institutions decide which forms of excellence deserve an award, who is eligible, and who receives a vote. The figure below brings those repeated decisions together to show which players have accumulated the most official recognition across their WNBA careers.

Source: Across the Timeline awards records. Visualization by Mariana Rosas.

The distribution is concentrated. The leading totals reach 33 and 30, while many players in this already elite group remain in the low teens. Awards build on one another: once a player becomes a recognized star, later ballots are read through an established reputation. The chart therefore records both performance and the durability of recognition.

What awards make visible

Career totals capture repeated excellence, but they privilege qualities that have a named category and a surviving record. Shooting, defense, rebounding, wins, and all-league selections are legible to the archive. Leadership, communication, screens, role flexibility, and the labor of making teammates better are much harder to count. Research on basketball performance also shows that winning depends on several offensive and defensive measures rather than one dominant statistic (Uysal et al. 2026).

For that reason, this page does not treat the ranking as a final list of the greatest players. It treats it as evidence about the league’s definition of greatness. As D’Ignazio and Klein argue, data categories reflect choices about what is worth counting. Official awards preserve important achievements, but they also narrow memory to the achievements the ballot knows how to name.
