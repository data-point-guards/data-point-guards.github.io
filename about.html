The Team

About This Project

Data Point Guards is a student digital humanities project created for Digital Humanities 101 at UCLA. We study WNBA success through attendance, draft position, career length, on-court performance, and awards. Placing those measures beside one another lets us ask when they agree, when they conflict, and whose value becomes visible when one measure is treated as more authoritative than another.

Johanna Drucker describes a digital humanities project as a movement from materials, through processing, to presentation. She also warns that technical choices at every stage carry interpretive and ethical consequences (Drucker 2021, 1–4). The following account makes our choices at all three levels explicit.

Level 1

Sources

Our principal born-digital source is Across the Timeline, an independently maintained public archive of women’s basketball records. We used its WNBA attendance, schedules and standings, drafts and draft lotteries, player statistics, awards and award votes, All-Star selections, GM surveys, and NCAA Division I rankings and tournament records. The archive covers the WNBA from its first season in 1997, allowing comparisons across teams, players, cities, and seasons. We supplemented those records with U.S. Census metropolitan-area data, official WNBA draft and award records, Basketball Reference season tables, eighteen scholarly sources, and credited photographs. The complete citations and annotations appear on the Sources page.

Why these sources

Across the Timeline was selected because it places many kinds of WNBA records in one searchable archive. That breadth supports our central comparison: official recognition, public attendance, draft expectations, career outcomes, and performance do not necessarily define success in the same way. Scholarly sources give us explanations that the numerical archive cannot supply, including research on popularity, decision-making bias, labor, gender, and the economics of women’s professional basketball.

What the source cannot settle

We do not treat the archive as a neutral or complete record. Its categories privilege events that institutions already count: games played, points scored, votes cast, players drafted, and seats filled. It contains far less about injuries, family decisions, contracts, coaching judgments, marketing, fan experience, or the reasons behind a trade or waiver. Michel-Rolph Trouillot locates historical silence in the making of sources, archives, narratives, and later historical significance; those silences are already present before we make a chart (Trouillot 1995, 26–29). Catherine D’Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein similarly argue that data must be read in context and through questions about power: who does the work, who benefits, and whose priorities are counted (D’Ignazio and Klein 2020, 17–18). These principles led us to state limitations beside our claims instead of presenting the dataset as the whole story.

Level 2

Processing

Each researcher began with a defined humanities question and selected only the relevant records from the larger archive. We standardized player, franchise, and season labels; checked missing or inconsistent values; filtered records by the period being studied; and calculated comparisons such as season totals, vote shares, ranks, career length, and changes in attendance. Spreadsheet work and Tableau supported cleaning, exploration, and early visualization. Final values were checked against the source records before being transferred into the website.

The published site uses a deliberately legible technical structure. Five completed team visualizations are preserved as exported figures so the website reproduces the exact labels, scales, annotations, and map design approved by the researchers. The sixth visualization — a five-season follow-up of the first twenty 2020 draft selections — stores its labels, team status, and 2025 win totals beside its Chart.js configuration. The league chronology is structured as semantic HTML and styled as a scroll-responsive timeline.

Why we processed the data this way

We chose a transparent, question-by-question workflow instead of an opaque automated pipeline because the project is small, interpretive, and intended to be checked by other students. Drucker describes processing as a potential “black box” and emphasizes that computational procedures always contain intellectual models (Drucker 2021, 1–4). Keeping the transformations simple makes our assumptions easier to audit. The cleaning and chart-selection guidance in Data + Design also shaped our practice of checking formats, labels, and missing values before deciding how much information each visualization should display.

This choice has a tradeoff: the final site is a documented snapshot, not a live connection to the archive. It loads reliably without a data server and preserves the values used in our argument, but a future season must be added and checked manually. Calculated measures also remain interpretations. For example, points measure scoring rather than total contribution, and a franchise’s most recent attendance season does not make every historical city directly comparable.

Level 3

Presentation

The project is presented as a static, responsive website built with semantic HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Six topic pages preserve the team’s six research questions: College to Pros, The Draft, Attendance, Awards, The League, and Greatness. Across those pages, the project presents five data visualizations, one map, and one timeline. A separate Data Critique examines the archive’s ontology, while this About page documents method and labor and the Sources page records evidence and image credits. The site can be hosted as ordinary files without a database or application server.

Our presentation places interpretation beside evidence. Visualizations appear inside the narrative rather than in a detached gallery; captions state the measure, time period, and clickable source; and the shared visual system uses consistent typography, spacing, and color. Interaction is limited to places where it adds information: draft-chart hover values, the scroll-responsive timeline, and expandable bibliography annotations. Responsive layouts, keyboard-accessible navigation, reduced-motion support, lazy-loaded images, and readable text widths were included so the argument remains usable across devices.

Why we presented the project this way

Drucker argues that presentation is not a neutral final wrapper: an interface creates hierarchy by deciding what is revealed, emphasized, or concealed (Drucker 2021, 1–4). We therefore kept the research questions visible, used one page per member’s topic, and linked every visualization to explanatory prose. A hand-built static site gave us more control over that hierarchy than a fixed template and made the project portable. The design still guides attention, so the Data Critique and full bibliography remain in the primary navigation rather than being hidden as technical appendices.
