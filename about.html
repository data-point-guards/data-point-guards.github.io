About This Project

Data Point Guards is a student digital humanities project created for Digital Humanities 101 at UCLA. Our project looks at how success is measured in the WNBA through attendance, market size, draft position, college achievement, player performance, league growth, and award recognition.

These measurements do not always tell the same story. A team in a large city does not automatically fill its arena, a high draft pick does not guarantee a successful career, and award voting may reflect more than individual statistics. By comparing these areas, we wanted to show that success in the WNBA cannot be explained by one number alone.

Our course described a digital humanities project through three main levels: sources, processing, and presentation. Sources are the materials used to build the project. Processing includes the choices made when selecting, organizing, comparing, and interpreting those materials. Presentation is how the findings are communicated to an audience through visualizations, written analysis, and the website itself.

Level 1

Sources

Our main dataset came from Across the Timeline, a public digital archive of women’s basketball records. We used its information on WNBA attendance, venues, standings, drafts, player statistics, awards, award voting, and NCAA tournament results. Since the archive covers the league from its first season in 1997 through recent seasons, it allowed us to compare teams, players, cities, and different periods in WNBA history.

We also used outside sources when our research questions required information that was not included in the main dataset. U.S. Census metropolitan-area data was used to compare the population of WNBA markets. Official WNBA records and Basketball Reference provided additional information about drafts, awards, players, teams, and league history. Scholarly articles gave us context about attendance, drafting, career length, player evaluation, labor conditions, media visibility, and the economics of women’s professional sports. Photographs were taken from credited sources such as Wikimedia Commons. Full citations for our data, research, and images appear on the Sources page.

Why We Chose These Sources

Across the Timeline was useful because it brings many different types of WNBA information together in one archive. This allowed us to connect topics that are often studied separately. For example, we could compare draft selections with later attendance, NCAA results with rookie performance, and award voting with player statistics.

However, numbers cannot explain every pattern on their own. Attendance data can show that a crowd increased, but it cannot fully explain the effects of ticket prices, marketing, media coverage, scheduling, or community support. Draft records show where a player was selected, but they do not explain every reason a team valued that player. Scholarly research helped us place the numerical patterns within larger discussions about opportunity, gender, labor, popularity, and recognition.

Limits and Silences in the Sources

We do not treat the dataset as a complete or neutral record of the WNBA. It mainly contains information that basketball organizations already record, such as points, wins, attendance, votes, transactions, and draft selections. It contains much less information about injuries, contracts, family decisions, coaching judgments, marketing budgets, discrimination, fan experiences, or the reasons behind trades, waivers, and career breaks.

Michel-Rolph Trouillot explains that silences enter history when sources are created, when records are placed into archives, when facts are used to build narratives, and when later audiences decide what is important. He argues that power is already present at the source because some experiences are recorded while others are not (Trouillot 1995, 26–29).

This applies to our project because the available categories influenced the questions we could answer. Statistics make scoring, attendance, and awards visible, while qualities such as leadership, teamwork, personal experience, and unequal opportunity are harder to measure. Our visualizations should therefore be understood as interpretations of the available data rather than complete explanations of WNBA history. We discuss these issues further on the Data Critique page.

Level 2

Processing

Each research question required a different selection and organization of the data. Rather than placing the entire archive into one visualization, team members focused on the records related to their individual topics.

For the Draft section, top-three draft selections were connected with franchise attendance figures. Attendance before and after the arrival of each player was compared to show the direction and size of the change. The unusual conditions of the 2020 WNBA bubble were explained instead of treating that season as a normal attendance year.

For the Attendance section, arena attendance and seating capacity were considered alongside metropolitan population. Markets were divided into three groups: small markets with fewer than one million people, medium markets with one to five million people, and large markets with more than five million people. Teams that played in different venues remained separate because the arenas did not have the same capacity. We used occupancy rate, or the percentage of available seats filled, so that smaller and larger arenas could be compared more fairly. Another visualization compared regular-season winning percentage with average home attendance across the three market groups.

For the College to Pros section, 2025 rookie points per game were compared with draft position. Color was used to show how far each player’s college team advanced in the NCAA tournament. This allowed us to examine how college success, draft opportunity, and early professional performance were connected.

For the Awards section, 2025 MVP voting points were organized by player. The written analysis compared the voting results with statistics such as scoring, assists, efficiency, and team success. For the League section, selected moments involving attendance, expansion, labor agreements, and professional opportunities were arranged chronologically with TimelineJS.

Tableau Public was used for the draft, attendance, college, and MVP visualizations. The historical timeline was made with TimelineJS.

Why We Processed the Data This Way

Nathan Yau explains that visual exploration should begin by asking what information is available and what the researcher wants to learn from it. The visual form should follow the question instead of being chosen only because it looks interesting (Yau 2013, 137–49).

We followed this approach by using different visual forms for different questions. A dumbbell chart makes the change between two attendance values easy to see. A map helps readers examine the relationship between teams and location. Scatterplots help show relationships between numerical variables, while bar charts make differences in voting totals easier to compare. A timeline was useful because league growth did not happen in a simple or uninterrupted direction.

These choices also have limits. Dividing markets into three groups makes the map easier to read, but cities within the same category can still be very different. Occupancy rate helps compare arenas, but it may hide unmet demand when a smaller arena sells out. A change in attendance after a draft selection does not prove that one player caused the change. Team performance, marketing, venue changes, and broader league growth may also matter. In the same way, rookie scoring and award voting represent only part of a player’s overall value.

We kept these limitations in the written analysis instead of presenting the visualizations as final or complete answers.

Level 3

Presentation

The project is presented as a responsive website built with HTML, CSS, and JavaScript and hosted through GitHub Pages. The main navigation includes Home, The Business, The Game, Data Critique, About, and Sources.

The research is divided into two larger paths. The Business contains Draft, Attendance, and League, which focus on audience demand, franchise growth, market conditions, and professional opportunities. The Game contains College to Pros, Awards, and Greatness, which focus on player development, performance, recognition, and the meaning of athletic greatness.

Most of the visualizations are embedded from Tableau Public. The league chronology is embedded from TimelineJS. Each visualization appears close to the written explanation of its question, findings, and limitations. This helps readers understand not only what the chart shows, but also why the comparison matters.

Why We Presented the Project This Way

We chose a narrative website instead of presenting the visualizations as a separate gallery because the charts need context. A chart can show a pattern, but the surrounding writing explains the variables, historical background, and limits of the conclusion. Dividing the project into Business and Game also allowed us to connect the growth of the league with the way individual players are evaluated and remembered.

The attendance map required additional choices. Todd Presner and David Shepard explain that maps use abstraction, scale, coordinates, perspective, and symbols to communicate relationships. They argue that maps make claims while also leaving out other possible ways of representing the same information (Presner and Shepard 2016, 201–2).

Our map is therefore more than a display of where teams are located. Dividing cities into population groups, keeping venues separate, and using occupancy rate creates an argument about the relationship between market size, arena capacity, and fan support. Because those choices simplify the real world, we explain them beside the map rather than presenting it as a neutral picture.

The website also includes responsive layouts, descriptive image text, keyboard-accessible navigation, a skip-to-content link, readable text widths, and reduced-motion support. These features were added so that the project would remain understandable and usable across different devices and methods of navigation.
