The League

How has the league grown — and for whom?

“How has the growth of the WNBA changed opportunities for women in sports over time?”

In 1997, eight teams played the WNBA's first season. The league nearly doubled by 2000, riding the optimism of the era — and then a decade of contraction followed. Miami and Portland folded in 2002, Cleveland in 2003, and Charlotte in 2007. The Houston Comets won the league's first four championships and still disappeared in 2008; the Sacramento Monarchs, champions in 2005, were gone a year later. Detroit's three-time champions were relocated to a smaller market. For fifteen years afterward, the league held at exactly twelve teams — a long plateau in which every roster spot was scarce and every franchise's survival was an achievement. Now the map is growing again: Golden State in 2025, followed by Toronto and Portland in 2026. The timeline below places selected moments from this history alongside changes in player opportunity, attendance, recognition, and working conditions.

Three decades of change

This timeline follows major moments in WNBA history, from the league's formation and first draft to changing player conditions, record attendance, and renewed expansion. Together, these events show that league growth has not followed a simple straight line.

Each new team creates roughly twelve roster positions, while contraction removes jobs and forces players to compete for space elsewhere. But opportunity is not only about the number of roster spots. It also depends on whether players have the compensation and working conditions needed to remain in the profession. For much of the league's history, many players spent the offseason competing overseas. The 2020 collective bargaining agreement began to change that structure by increasing compensation and improving working conditions (WNBA and WNBPA 2020).

What the timeline shows

The history of the WNBA has not followed a simple path of steady growth. The league created new professional opportunities in 1997, but those opportunities have always been shaped by draft decisions, limited roster space, working conditions, franchise stability, and public attention.

The timeline also shows that different measurements tell different parts of the story. Attendance records can show growing interest, but the 2020 season demonstrates why numbers need historical context. Awards and lists such as the W25 preserve certain players in the league's official history, while the draft influences which players receive an opportunity to build a professional career.

Recent expansion and record attendance suggest that the league is entering another period of growth. However, its history shows that growth should not be measured only by the number of teams or spectators. It should also be measured by the opportunities, working conditions, and long-term stability available to players and franchises.
