The Women's National Basketball Association (WNBA) is still a relatively new professional sports league compared with leagues such as the NBA, NFL, MLB, and NHL. Its first season was played in 1997, which gives us the opportunity to examine the league from its beginning to the present. For our project, we look at several ways success can be measured in the WNBA, including attendance, draft position, length of a player's career, performance, and awards. Our findings suggest that commonly used measures of success in the WNBA do not always tell the same story. Metropolitan population does not consistently predict attendance, award recognition does not appear to depend solely on scoring, and career outcomes vary considerably depending on where a player is selected in the draft. We collected our data from Across the Timeline. The website provides historical WNBA records on attendance, drafts, awards, standings, and player statistics, which we use to compare teams and players across different cities and seasons.

Other researchers have asked similar questions before us, and they often find the same thing. Economists who study basketball find that the statistics people pay the most attention to, like points scored, do not really show how much a player helps the team win. They also find that the experts who judge players rely too much on a player's early reputation, so they make the same mistakes in the draft again and again. Research on attendance is not as clear. Some studies find that star players bring more fans to games, but other studies find that what brings fans to a WNBA game is not the same as what brings them to an NBA game, so the two leagues should not be compared. Other scholars look at things outside the numbers. They say that women's basketball has always been shaped by the way the league is marketed, and that data is never neutral because someone always decides what is worth counting. What has not been done yet is putting all of these findings together to see whether the records on attendance, drafts, careers, and awards tell the same story.

We are working on how the WNBA measures success because we want to find out whether these different measures agree with each other, so that we can help others understand that a number is never only a number. Someone has to decide what gets counted, and those decisions shape who is remembered and who is forgotten. This matters outside of basketball too. The WNBA is still a young league, and people are still arguing about what its players are worth. Those arguments use the same numbers we study here, such as attendance, awards, and salaries. If the numbers are incomplete or unfair, then the arguments built on them are unfair as well. Looking closely at the records of one league is a way of asking a much larger question about any place where people are counted, ranked, and remembered.

Six chapters, one question

Each chapter takes up one room of the same house: the leap from college fame to a professional job, the draft where futures are handed out, the arenas where cities show up or stay home, the ballots that crown the best, the map of a league that grew and shrank and grew again, and finally the archive itself — the quiet keeper of who gets remembered.
