This is the story of the WNBA — America’s professional basketball league for women.

The Women’s National Basketball Association began play in 1997, making it a relatively young league compared with the NBA, NFL, MLB, and NHL. Its development over nearly three decades provides an opportunity to examine how success is measured as a professional sports league grows. For this project, we compare several measures of WNBA success, including attendance, metropolitan population, draft position, career length, player performance, and award recognition.

Our visualizations suggest that these measures are connected, but they do not always produce the same conclusions. Metropolitan population does not consistently predict how successfully teams fill their arenas, players selected early in the draft generally have longer careers but do not all experience the same outcomes, and award voting reflects several forms of contribution rather than scoring alone. We collected our data from Across the Timeline, a website that provides historical WNBA records on attendance, drafts, standings, awards, voting results, and player statistics. Because the data covers both team and player outcomes across multiple seasons, it allows us to compare different definitions of success within the same league.

Researchers have examined many of these measures, but they often study them separately. Some factors commonly associated with demand in men’s professional sports do not appear to explain WNBA attendance in the same way (Agha and Berri, 2023). Studies show that metropolitan population, income, winning percentage, and star players may be less consistent predictors of attendance than expected. Our attendance visualizations generally support this conclusion because teams in the largest metropolitan areas did not always fill a greater percentage of their available seats. Another study analyzes attendance from a different perspective by examining the interests and behavior of WNBA spectators (Zhang, 2003). The researchers find that interest in other sports can overlap with interest in women’s basketball, suggesting that nearby professional teams do not necessarily compete directly with the WNBA for every fan.

Research on player evaluation also questions traditional methods of evaluating individual players, arguing that scoring and other highly visible statistics can receive more attention than other contributions to winning (Berri, 1999). This idea is relevant to our comparisons of player performance, draft position, and award voting. At the same time, public recognition in women’s basketball is influenced by marketing and cultural expectations as well as athletic performance (Banet-Weiser, 1999). Overall, the research suggests that success in the WNBA cannot be understood through one measure alone. Most of these studies focus on attendance, player performance, drafting, or recognition as separate topics. Our project looks at these areas together to see whether they tell a similar story.

This comparison matters because decisions about teams and players are often based on measures such as market size, draft position, scoring averages, and attendance. Looking at several measures together helps show when they agree and when they point in different directions. For example, a large metropolitan area may not guarantee high arena occupancy, an early draft selection may improve a player’s chances of having a longer career without guaranteeing individual success, and a player can contribute to winning in ways that are not fully represented by scoring.

By comparing team, market, and player data, we hope to help you understand that success in the WNBA is multidimensional. This broader understanding can lead to better interpretations of the league’s growth and more informed judgments about which teams, players, and achievements receive investment and recognition.
