The Women's National Basketball Association (WNBA) is still a relatively new professional sports league compared with leagues such as the NBA, NFL, MLB, and NHL. Its first season was played in 1997, which gives us the opportunity to examine the league from its beginning to the present. For our project, we look at several ways success can be measured in the WNBA, including attendance, draft position, length of a player's career, performance, and awards. Our findings suggest that commonly used measures of success in the WNBA do not always tell the same story.

Metropolitan population does not consistently predict attendance, award recognition does not appear to depend solely on scoring, and career outcomes vary considerably depending on where a player is selected in the draft. We collected our data from Across the Timeline. The website provides historical WNBA records on attendance, drafts, awards, standings, and player statistics, which we use to compare teams and players across different cities and seasons.

Other researchers have asked similar questions before us, and they often find the same thing. Economists who study basketball find that the statistics people pay the most attention to, like points scored, do not really show how much a player helps the team win. They also find that the experts who judge players rely too much on a player's early reputation, so they make the same mistakes in the draft again and again. (Berri 1999; Berger and Daumann 2021)

Research on attendance is not as clear. Some studies find that star players bring more fans to games, but other studies find that what brings fans to a WNBA game is not the same as what brings them to an NBA game, so the two leagues should not be compared. Other scholars look at things outside the numbers. They say that women's basketball has always been shaped by the way the league is marketed, and that data is never neutral because someone always decides what is worth counting. What has not been done yet is putting all of these findings together to see whether the records on attendance, drafts, careers, and awards tell the same story. (Humphreys and Johnson 2020; Agha and Berri 2023; Banet-Weiser 1999; D’Ignazio and Klein 2020)

We are looking at these different measures of WNBA success together, attendance, draft position, career length, performance, and awards, because we want to find out whether they agree on what “success” really means, or whether each one tells a different story. This matters because the WNBA is at a turning point right now. Attendance has almost doubled since 2019, new expansion teams are joining the league, and the choices being made now about which cities to invest in, which players to build around, and which achievements to reward will shape the league for a long time. (WNBA 2024 season report; WNBA expansion FAQ)

If these measures disagree with each other, the biggest cities may not draw the biggest crowds, the highest draft picks may not always be the best pick, and awards may reward fame more than the quiet work that actually wins games. When that happens, trusting just one measure can lead you to the wrong answer. By putting these measures side by side instead of studying them one at a time, we hope to help others understand that “success” in a growing league is not one single thing. For a sport still fighting for investment and attention, being honest about what the data does and does not show affects real outcomes: which teams survive, which players get valued, and whose work gets noticed.

Six chapters, one question

Each chapter takes up one room of the same house: the leap from college fame to a professional job, the draft where futures are handed out, the arenas where cities show up or stay home, the ballots that crown the best, the map of a league that grew and shrank and grew again, and finally the archive itself — the quiet keeper of who gets remembered. Read them in order, or open any one; each stands on its own.
