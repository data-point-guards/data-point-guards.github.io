The Draft

What does a top draft pick really buy?

“Five seasons later, where were the first twenty players selected in the 2020 WNBA Draft, and how many games did their 2025 teams win?”

Draft order is the league’s clearest public forecast of future value. To test how that forecast ages, we followed the first twenty selections from the 2020 WNBA Draft into the 2025 season. Five years is long enough to move beyond a rookie adjustment while remaining within the requested five-to-ten-year window.

Where the 2020 top 20 stood five seasons later
2025 regular-season wins for each active player’s team; gray labels mark players without a 2025 WNBA appearance. Team wins describe competitive context, not individual performance.

Sources: 2020 WNBA Draft board, 2025 player appearances, and 2025 final standings. Team abbreviations identify the player’s 2025 team.

Only five of the first twenty selections appeared in 2025 WNBA statistics: Sabrina Ionescu, Satou Sabally, Tyasha Harris, Kitija Laksa, and Joyner Holmes. Their teams ranged from ten wins in Dallas to thirty in Las Vegas. The spread is the important result: an early selection neither guarantees a place in the league five seasons later nor places a surviving player in the same competitive situation.

What the chart can and cannot show

The graph deliberately separates career persistence from team context. A player receives no personal credit for every team win, and a player absent from 2025 statistics is not automatically a failed pick. Injury, retirement, overseas play, roster limits, and team decisions can all remove a player from the season table. Research likewise finds that teams often value college scoring and tournament visibility even when those signals are weak predictors of professional performance (Harris and Berri 2015).

The comparison therefore answers a narrower and more defensible question than the original draft-night hype: how durable was the league’s ranking, and what team environment surrounded the players who remained? The result shows why draft position should be treated as an expectation rather than a career verdict. The limits of the available records are discussed further in our Data Critique.
