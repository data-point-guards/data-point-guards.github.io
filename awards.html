The Ballots

Do the awards go to the best players?

“How well do awards and expert opinions in the WNBA reflect players’ actual performance on the court, and what might explain the gaps between recognition and reality?”

A season produces box scores and ballots. Box scores record what happened on the court; ballots record how a panel of sportswriters and broadcasters interpreted that performance. The 2025 MVP vote makes the distance between those two records visible because every placement was converted into points: ten for first, seven for second, five for third, three for fourth, and one for fifth (WNBA Communications 2025).

The ballot has a steep hierarchy. A’ja Wilson received 657 points, Napheesa Collier 534, and Alyssa Thomas 391; the total then fell to 180 for Allisha Gray and 93 for Kelsey Mitchell. Eight other players shared only seventeen points. Recognition was therefore not spread evenly across many strong seasons. It concentrated around a small group on whom voters largely agreed.

What the gap means

The chart measures recognition, not a complete ranking of performance. Wilson led the league in scoring and received 51 of 72 first-place votes, but voters also consider defense, playmaking, team success, availability, and narrative. That broader judgment can be defensible while still reflecting reputation and prior expectations. Research on basketball decision-making shows that perceived value and measured production do not always align (Berri 1999).

Because the figure shows voting points alone, it cannot prove why one player received more recognition than another. It does show where the official record narrows: most of the league disappears from the ballot. Media visibility and the cultural construction of WNBA stardom help explain why that narrowing deserves scrutiny (Banet-Weiser 1999). Awards preserve a season for the future, so evaluating the ballot is also an audit of how the league writes its own history.
