Annotated Bibliography

Harris, Jill, and David J. Berri. “Predicting the WNBA Draft: What Matters Most from College Performance?” International Journal of Sport Finance 10, no. 4 (2015): 299–309. https://doi.org/10.1177/155862351501000401.

This article looks at what WNBA teams pay attention to when drafting college players, and if those things actually show how good the player will be in the WNBA. The authors studied 128 players drafted from 2010 to 2013. They looked at college stats such as points, assists, shooting percentage, height, conference, and if the player made the Final Four. One thing that stood out to me was that Final Four experience helped a player move up almost six draft spots. Scoring also mattered a lot for getting drafted higher. But being drafted higher did not explain professional success that much. Draft position explained only around 6 percent of career performance using the measure in the article. Shooting efficiency was actually more useful for predicting first-year performance, even though teams seemed to focus more on total scoring. This relates to my question because NCAA success can help a player get noticed, but it does not mean the same success will continue in the WNBA. The study is a little limited because it only uses four draft years and 128 players. Also, it mostly uses making the Final Four as the measurement of tournament success, so it does not show the full NCAA tournament experience.

Jepsen, Christopher. “Determinants of Career Exits and Career Breaks in Women’s Professional Basketball.” Journal of Sports Economics 24, no. 8 (2023): 1055–75. https://doi.org/10.1177/15270025231190647.

Jepsen studies why WNBA players leave the league or take a break from playing. The article uses data from 1997 through 2019 and includes 923 players, which is a much larger time period than the first article. A lot of the article talks about race and international players, but the part that connects most to my question is productivity and draft round. More productive players were less likely to leave, and first-round picks were also less likely to have a career exit or break. First-round picks had about an 8 percentage-point lower chance of permanently leaving and around a 7 percentage-point lower chance of taking a break. This made me think that being valued highly in the draft can help a player stay in the league longer. But the first article says draft position does not predict performance very well, so maybe teams give first-round picks more time or more chances. The article does not actually prove that though, it is more of a possible explanation. One weakness is that the article puts many reasons for leaving together. Injury, pregnancy, overseas basketball, retirement, or personal reasons can all be counted as an exit or career break. So it tells us who leaves, but not always why they left.

Tramer, Joseph S., Lafi S. Khalil, Toufic R. Jildeh, Mohammad Sattar, Alexander Ziedas, Muhammad J. Abbas, Patricia A. Kolowich, and Kelechi R. Okoroha. “Association of Prior Anterior Cruciate Ligament Tear with Decreased Career Longevity in Women’s National Basketball Association.” Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine 9, no. 6 (2021): 23259671211009248. https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671211009248.

This article is about WNBA players who had an ACL tear before they entered the league. The researchers compared 42 players who had ACL reconstruction with 42 similar players who did not have a prior ACL injury. They matched the players by age, position, and body mass index. The players with an ACL injury had shorter WNBA careers, averaging 4.8 seasons compared with 8.1 seasons for the control group. They also started less games and played fewer minutes. Another result that surprised me was that 11 of the ACL players only played one WNBA season, while none of the control players only played one. Even with that, their player efficiency ratings were not significantly different. So when they were actually playing, their performance could still be similar, but they had less playing time and did not stay in the league as long. This is probably the most direct article for my injury question. It shows that an injury before the WNBA can affect career length even when a player is still talented enough to perform well. A limitation is that the study used public information for injury history, and only 42 injured players were included. The authors also could not say for sure that the ACL tear was the only reason for the shorter career because rehab, team decisions, or other injuries could also be part of it.

Humphreys, Brad R., and Candon Johnson. “The Effect of Superstars on Game Attendance: Evidence from the NBA.” Journal of Sports Economics 21, no. 2 (2020): 152–75. https://doi.org/10.1177/1527002519885441.

Humphreys and Johnson argue that superstar players generate attendance externalities that extend beyond their individual contribution to team success, meaning they draw crowds not only for their own team but for opponents who never pay their salary. They test this using a censored normal estimator on more than 36,000 NBA games from the 1981–82 through 2013–14 seasons, correcting for the fact that sellouts truncate observed attendance and controlling for team quality, point spreads, arena novelty effects, and team and season fixed effects. Their most useful contribution for my research is the distinction they draw between two models of stardom: Larry Bird’s attendance effect tracks his on-court production, while Michael Jordan’s and LeBron James’s track their All-Star vote totals rather than their performance metrics. This matters for my question because it means an elite draft pick could raise attendance through two entirely different channels, and only one of them requires the player to actually be good. Notably, metropolitan population was statistically insignificant in every model they estimated, which supports my group’s finding that market size is a weak predictor of attendance.

Jane, Wen-Jhan. “The Effect of Star Quality on Attendance Demand: The Case of the National Basketball Association.” Journal of Sports Economics 17, no. 4 (2016): 396–417. https://doi.org/10.1177/1527002514530405.

Jane argues that star players raise attendance at NBA games for both home and visiting teams and, more pointedly, that this pull comes from a star’s popularity rather than from on-court talent. Because earlier studies reached mixed conclusions, he attributes the disagreement to how star is measured, and so tests five different definitions across 1,813 games from the 2010–11 and 2011–12 seasons: top-30 performers, top-30 earners, two versions of All-Star selection, and total All-Star votes. He uses a Tobit model, converting attendance into a percentage of each arena’s capacity so that sellouts, 47% of his sample, do not bias the estimates downward. The appearance of a star raises game attendance by roughly 3.3% to 8.1%, and one home star drawing a million All-Star votes adds about 1,390 fans. His most important move for my project comes when he enters talent and popularity into the same regression: the performance measures lose significance while the All-Star (popularity) measures stay strong, leading him to conclude that star effects from talent tend to be dominated by popularity. This matters directly to my question, because it means a high draft pick could lift attendance simply by becoming famous, independent of whether she actually improves her team.

Massey, Cade, and Richard H. Thaler. “The Loser’s Curse: Decision Making and Market Efficiency in the National Football League Draft.” Management Science 59, no. 7 (2013): 1479–95. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1120.1657.

Massey and Thaler argue that NFL teams overvalue high draft picks, and that this mispricing survives despite enormous financial stakes and decades of opportunity to learn. Using 407 draft-day trades from 1983 to 2008, they estimate the market value of each pick, then calculate each drafted player’s surplus value by subtracting his salary from the market price of an equivalent veteran. They found that surplus value increases across the first round, so the last pick in round one delivers more value to its team than the first pick, and teams that trade down gain an average of 5.4 starts per season without sacrificing Pro Bowl selections. This source is essential to my research because my question assumes that securing a top-three pick is a meaningful competitive and commercial asset, and Massey and Thaler give me a rigorous reason to test that assumption rather than accept it. The obvious limitation is domain: the NFL fields 22 starters, and the authors themselves invoke this roster size when dismissing a superstar explanation for their results, explicitly contrasting football with basketball, where a single player can transform a team. That contrast is exactly why the paper is worth testing against WNBA data rather than importing wholesale.

Agha, Nola, and David Berri. “Demand for Basketball: A Comparison of the WNBA and NBA.” International Journal of Sport Finance 18, no. 1 (2023): 35–44. https://doi.org/10.32731/ijsf.181.022023.03.

Agha and Berri examine whether it is reasonable to compare consumer demand for the WNBA with demand for the NBA. They argue that comparing the two leagues directly is unfair because the NBA has been around much longer and has more funding and media coverage than the WNBA. To make the comparison more balanced, they look at WNBA attendance from 2004 to 2017 and compare it with NBA attendance during both the NBA’s earlier years and that same period. They find that different factors influence demand in each league. In the WNBA, previous championship teams often produced greater attendance, while county population, income, winning percentage, and star players were not significant factors. This article is useful to my research because it supports my previous finding that market population does not have a clear relationship with WNBA attendance. It also suggests that a team’s championship history may help explain why some cities support their teams more strongly. However, the article does not directly examine competition from other professional sports teams.

Johnson, Lauren M., and Hannah H. Bo. “Exploring Consumer Demand in Women’s Professional Basketball.” Journal of Marketing Development and Competitiveness 17, no. 3 (2023): 52–61.

Johnson and Bo examine the features of live WNBA games that may influence future ticket sales. From previous studies, they identify five traditional demand factors: the quality of the home team, quality of the opposing team, game promotion, economic considerations, and schedule convenience. However, they argue that these traditional factors do not fully explain the WNBA’s appeal. The authors then use a Stimulus-Organism-Response (SOR) model and propose two additional factors: interactive activity and advocacy programming. Interactive activity refers to the different ways fans can become involved during a WNBA event, such as meeting players, entering contests, participating in youth activities, or connecting with the team online. Advocacy programming refers to the WNBA’s involvement in social causes through events such as Pride nights, charity partnerships, and programs that support women and the local community. Johnson and Bo believe these features may improve fans’ attitudes toward the live experience, increase satisfaction, and encourage additional consumption. This article is useful to my research because it shows that the population of a home team’s metropolitan area may not be the only reason for its attendance numbers. Factors such as ticket prices, team’s performance, player popularity, scheduling, marketing, community involvement, and the overall game experience could also help explain why teams in similarly sized markets have different attendance. It also made me consider why having people interested in a team does not always result in higher attendance or revenue. One weakness of the article is that the authors only propose a model. They do not use their own attendance data or survey results to prove that these factors cause more people to attend games.

Zhang, James J., Lori Pennington-Gray, Daniel P. Connaughton, Jessica R. Braunstein, Matthew H. Ellis, Eddie T. C. Lam, and Debbie Williamson. “Understanding Women’s Professional Basketball Game Spectators: Sociodemographics, Game Consumption, and Entertainment Options.” Sport Marketing Quarterly 12, no. 4 (2003): 228–43. https://doi.org/10.1177/106169340301200405.

Zhang and his coauthors examine how the demographics and entertainment interests of WNBA spectators relate to game attendance. They surveyed 2,048 spectators at five home games of one WNBA team. They found that characteristics such as gender, age, income, education, occupation, and household size were connected to game consumption. Women, older spectators, people with higher incomes, and those with higher education levels generally attended more games. The study also found that people who attended other professional and amateur sporting events were slightly more likely to attend WNBA games. However, these entertainment interests explained very little of the difference in attendance. This article is useful to my research because it suggests that other professional sports may not always compete directly with the WNBA, since some spectators attend both WNBA games and other sporting events. The study also shows that the characteristics of a team’s local audience may help explain why some cities provide stronger support than others. However, the study only examines spectators of one successful WNBA team and does not compare different cities or count the number of professional teams in each market. The data are also from more than twenty years ago, so the current WNBA audience may be different.

Banet-Weiser, Sarah. “Hoop Dreams: Professional Basketball and the Politics of Race and Gender.” Journal of Sport and Social Issues 23, no. 4 (1999): 403–20. https://doi.org/10.1177/0193723599234004.

Banet-Weiser argues that professional basketball does not only show athletic talent, but also sells particular images of race and gender, and that the WNBA in its early years was marketed around a safe, family-friendly, and traditionally feminine image. She uses cultural analysis of the league’s marketing, advertising, and media coverage to show how the NBA and the new WNBA were represented to the public in very different ways. This is important because it reminds us that recognition in sports is shaped by culture and marketing, and not only by what happens on the court. For my research, this source adds a humanistic side to my question, because it suggests that who gets seen as “great” or valuable can depend on image and expectation instead of performance alone. It helps me argue that the gap between awards and reality is partly about who the league and its audience are taught to value.

Berger, Tobias, and Frank Daumann. “Anchoring Bias in the Evaluation of Basketball Players: A Closer Look at NBA Draft Decision-Making.” Managerial and Decision Economics 42, no. 5 (2021): 1248–62. https://doi.org/10.1002/mde.3305.

Berger and Daumann argue that the experts who evaluate basketball players suffer from an “anchoring bias,” which means they stay too close to the early pre-draft rankings and do not adjust enough to a player’s real ability. They study NBA draft decisions and compare them with the players’ amateur rankings (the RSCI rank), and they find that managers systematically over-value and under-value certain players because of that first anchor. This is important because it shows that expert opinion in basketball is not fully rational, and that decisions made by people in power can carry a hidden error that repeats again and again. For my research, this helps me explain why gaps appear between recognition and reality, because if experts anchor on reputation and early rankings, then awards and votes may follow the same bias. It connects directly to the part of my question about the WNBA GM survey and what its mistakes reveal about the people in “power.”

Berri, David J. “Who Is ‘Most Valuable’? Measuring the Player’s Production of Wins in the National Basketball Association.” Managerial and Decision Economics 20, no. 8 (1999): 411–27. https://doi.org/10.1002/1099-1468(199912)20:8<411::AID-MDE957>3.0.CO;2-G.

Berri argues that a player’s real value should be measured by how much that player actually contributes to team wins, and not by how many points he scores or by who the media decides to vote for. To show this, he builds an econometric model that links individual box-score statistics to team wins, and then uses it to calculate each player’s “production of wins” across many NBA seasons. This is important because it proves, with numbers, that the most famous and highest-voted players are not always the ones who help their team win the most, so the popular awards can be misleading. For my research, this source gives me a clear way to think about the gap between recognition and real performance, because it shows that “most valuable” can be defined by data instead of by reputation. It supports my argument that WNBA award votes may reward scoring and fame more than the quieter work, like defense, that actually wins games.

Ettienne, Lerae. “It’s Time to Pay Up, the Justification for Higher Salaries for WNBA Players: An Analysis of the WNBA’s Success and Employing Mediation between the WNBA and NBA to Leverage Future Success.” Pepperdine Dispute Resolution Law Journal 19, no. 2 (2019): 175–200. https://digitalcommons.pepperdine.edu/drlj/vol19/iss2/2/.

Examining the structural and financial relationship between the WNBA and NBA leagues, Lerae Ettienne is able to argue for significantly underpaid members that make up the WNBA rosters. The crux of Etienne's argument rests upon the ideology that there is a lack of respect for women's professional basketball that is enforced by the NBA’s control. The article pulls a multi-lens of data to build their argument; such as growth metrics for comparison, data consisting of revenue and wage statistics, and even viewership ratings and holdings. This informative text is critical to look at the structural framework that is the current state of the WNBA and offers mediation for solving such disputes of the prevalent equity crisis. This article is an excellent resource for reference because it traces the WNBA evolution and highlights the disparities that are placed upon women in the athletic field and highlights that there still is work to be down when it comes to closing this gap, even while strides have been made to grant women more opportunities as they step into this expanding career.

Minuci, Eduardo, and Zachary Rodriguez. “Determinants of Contracts and Wages in the Women’s National Basketball Association.” SSRN working paper, May 13, 2026. https://ssrn.com/abstract=6763458.

In this working journal by Eduardo Minuci and Zachary Rodriguez, the authors aim to unearth the economic landscape of the WNBA league during the years 2017 through 2024. The writers contend that compensation for WNBA players rest on institutional agreements rather than individual on-court performances and results alone. This introduces a central issue with wage inequality that is analyzed using data containing the contracts of 134 league players and performance metrics that measure box scores. This current work has large weight as a resource because it is of the first articles that perform regression analysis on the 2020 bubble seasons between the WNBA and NBA to highlight the imbalances that are structured within the sports labor market, and further offers insight about women basketball players autonomy within the league. The article helps our research by divulging players' attempt towards autonomy by favoring shorter contract lengths which is used as a technique to re-negotiate for better terms as the league continues to expand and grow.

Zimmer, Timothy E., Allison Snyder, and Ian I. Zimmer. “WNBA Attendance and the Clark/Reese Effect.” Economics Bulletin 45, no. 2 (2025): 780–91. http://www.accessecon.com/Pubs/EB/2025/Volume45/EB-25-V45-I2-P68.pdf.

The article explores the attendance of the 2024 WNBA season and tries to justify the phenomenon that catapulted viewership. The main takeaway posits that the responsibility stems from an organic growth and interest in the sport in addition to the induction of highly popularized rookies such as Caitlin Clark and Angel Resse. This journal implements a least squares approach on a panel dataset of individual game statistics ranging from the start of the season in 2018 to half of the 2024 season, and it excludes the data collected from the 2020 “bubble” season. The entry by Zimmer et. al. is significant because it offers empirical evidence that can help identify whether the WNBA’s growth and interest capital is a passing “fad” or cultural shift. We are able to embed such findings within our narrative as it explores the contingencies which drive box office demand that lay within team success and personal stardom. The text states this surge paves way for expansion opportunities and globalizing the sport which opens more opportunities for women basketball players branding.

Lorenzo Calvo, Jorge, Rafael Manuel Navarro Barragán, Carlos Serrano Luengo, and Guillermo Muñoz Andradas. “Comparison between the WNBA and WEL: Differences and Key Performance Indicators in Top Women Basketball Leagues.” Retos 63 (2025): 886–95. https://doi.org/10.47197/retos.v63.107098.

The article, “Comparison between the WNBA and WEL: Differences and Key Performance Indicators in Top Women Basketball League” by Jorge Lorenzo Calvo compares the Women’s National Basketball Association (WNBA) and the Euroleague Women (WEL) it see the key performance indicators that show the success for each league. In the analysis it gives game statistics from 2018-2019 within the regular season and playoffs from the WNBA and WEL. The articles compares many factors such as points, rebounds, assists, etc and when comparing the main ideas is checking out what is most important to win a game. The key finding is that although the WNBA and WEL are top women’s basketball leagues there are different statistics and styles that contribute to their success. In the study it is shown that team success is influenced by multiple performance indicators that are associated with winning in the WNBA. The top three factors that predict the success of both the WNBA and WEL are points, defensive rebounds, and total rebounds. The main factor that is looked into the WNBA success of the percentage of three point shooting. This goes back to the research questions by showing what statistics show the greatness is shown by multiple performances. A strength in this article is that it is largely sampled 342 games and 684 team performance in the WNBA and WEL and is found that 89.3% of games were classified as statistical models which helps to indicate the greatness beyond individual awards. When it comes to weakness the author does apply that playoff samples are limited and it’s challenging to indicate the most important variable during the postseason.

Moss, Hollye, Bryan Schaffer, Mariano Garrido-López, Yue Hillon, AJ Grube, and Debra Burke. “Applying the Four Factors to the WNBA and Exploring Shared Leadership as a Fifth Factor.” Journal of Behavioral and Applied Management 25, no. 1 (2025): 36–44. https://doi.org/10.21818/001c.133764.

In the article, “Applying the Four Factors to the WNBA and Exploring Shared Leadership as a Fifth Factor” by Hollye Moss, examines if Dean Oliver’s four factors of basketball success in the WNBA are based on shooting, turnovers, rebounding, or free throws. The author also mentions if leadership should be considered as a fifth factor that implies the success of the team. The key finding shows that shooting is the strongest predictor when it comes to winning, meanwhile overtime rebounding has become more important and turnovers have become less influential. When it comes to leadership the teams being able to be motivated and working together make a great impact on the four factor model. This implies to the research questions because it measures how greatness is measured on women’s basketball by providing statistical evidence in which game factors contribute most wins. The article gives a table showing field goals accounted for 42% of team success making shooting the most important factor. Following the turnover at 30%, rebounds at 17% and free throws 12%. It is important since it demonstrates the winning from the overall team and not individual scores. One strength is that 23 seasons of the WNBA data from (1997 to 2019) are given to identify long term trends. One limitation is that there isn’t recent data from 2020 and recent which gives a disadvantage to see the impact newer players have established.

Uysal, Alp Aslan, Kevin Carroll, Alan Huebner, Anna Krush, and Michael Henry Stone. “Quantifying the Impact of Basketball Performance Metrics on Winning in Professional and Collegiate Basketball.” Scientific Journal of Sport and Performance 5, no. 2 (2026): 298. https://doi.org/10.55860/DHNF1439.

The article, “Quantifying the Impact of basketball performance metrics on winning in professional and collegiate basketball” by Alp Aslan Uysal, examines what basketball performance metrics have the greatest impact on winning percentage in professional and college basketball. The research gives an overview from 2014 to 2024 giving a more recent look from the NBA, WNBA, NCAA and showing which offensive and defensive statistics show what is associated with greatness and success. The key findings shows that three point shooting percentages have a strong relationship for wins in the NBA as in contrast to the WNBA and NCAA blocked shots is the strongest predictor of wins. As for the NBA it is more offense oriented as in the WNBA the importance is more on the balance between offensive and defensive performance. The findings relate to the research question because it suggests that WNBA is the most defensive oriented league meaning that the blocked shots, steals, defensive rating and rebounds all show a significant relationship when it comes to wins. The datasets focused only on ranking and awards that show the greatness of women's basketball that is measured using a broader set of performance metrics. One strong point of this article is that it gives ten years of statistical data from multiple basketball leagues in women, men, and college. One limitation is that it is not mainly focused on the WNBA creating a challenge to see the league's main performance and valuable data.
